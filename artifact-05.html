ARTIFACT 05

The Readiness Question: Scoring an AI Project Before You Build It

AI Strategy Decision Tool Capstone Synthesis

Four artifacts in this portfolio asked how machine learning works, how it is built, how it is trusted, and how it breaks. This one asks the question a decision-maker actually has to answer: given a specific project, is it ready? Score a real one below across data foundation, explainability, measurement, and governance, and see which dimension is holding it back.

Score a project ↓ Download the scorecard

Introduction

There is a gap between understanding how machine learning works and knowing whether a particular project should go ahead. The first is a knowledge problem and this portfolio has spent four artifacts on it. The second is a judgment problem, and it is the one that costs money: a team can correctly explain gradient descent, name every activation function, and still ship a model that nobody can audit, on data nobody checked, measured against a metric that hides the failure that matters.

The pattern showed up repeatedly across the earlier artifacts. A cancer classifier that is 99.6 percent accurate and catches almost no cancer. A recommendation engine that quietly degrades as its audience changes. A loan model that is defensible in aggregate and indefensible for the individual it just denied. In every one of those cases the modeling was not the weak point. The surrounding conditions were.

This final artifact turns those conditions into something a person can use in a meeting. It is a scorecard: twelve questions across four dimensions, scored live in the browser, producing a readiness rating and a named weakest link. It is also a look back at what the five artifacts add up to, because the four dimensions in the scorecard are not invented for this page. Each one is the thing an earlier artifact was really about.

Description

This artifact has three parts. The first is the AI Readiness Scorecard, an interactive tool built from scratch in plain JavaScript. The visitor answers twelve questions about a real or planned AI project, three in each of four dimensions: data foundation, explainability, measurement, and governance and monitoring. Each answer scores zero, one, or two points. The panel updates live with a total out of twenty-four, a per-dimension breakdown, a readiness band, and a callout naming the single weakest dimension with a concrete next step and a link to the artifact in this portfolio that covers it.

The second part is a written breakdown of the four dimensions: what each one buys a team, what skipping it costs, and what that cost looks like specifically in a finance or business setting. The third part is a short retrospective tracing the throughline across all five artifacts, from the history of the field to a working chatbot to a neuron simulator to six failure exhibits to this scorecard, and a downloadable one-page version of the scorecard for use away from the browser.

Objective

Three goals. First, convert the recurring lesson of this portfolio, that machine learning projects fail on their conditions rather than on their math, into a tool that produces a decision rather than an explanation. Second, close the portfolio as a portfolio: give a visitor arriving at the final artifact a reason to go back into the earlier four, by linking every weak score directly to the artifact that addresses it. Third, respond to feedback received on the previous artifact, which asked for measurable outcomes, by making measurement one of the four scored dimensions rather than a closing paragraph, and by treating the score itself as the measurable output the visitor takes away.

Weakest link: answer at least one question in each dimension to see which one is holding the project back.

The scoring is deliberately blunt. A project is treated as bounded by its weakest dimension, not by its average, because that is how these systems actually fail: strong modeling does not compensate for unchecked data, and a high total with one dimension near zero is a project with a specific problem rather than a good project overall.

The Four Dimensions

The dimensions are not a generic maturity model. Each one is the argument an earlier artifact in this portfolio was making, restated as something a team can be scored on.

Data Foundation

Class balance, group differences in outcomes, and the origin of the labels. These are the three questions that decide whether a model has learned the task or learned the shape of the dataset it was handed.

Cost of skipping it: a fraud model trained on a book where fraud is 0.4 percent of transactions can reach 99.6 percent accuracy by approving everything. The number on the dashboard is excellent and the losses are unchanged.

Explainability

Not whether the team understands the architecture, but whether anyone can account for one specific decision to the person on the other side of it. Per-decision attribution is a capability you build in advance or do not have when you need it.

Cost of skipping it: under adverse-action requirements, a lender that cannot say why an applicant was denied has a compliance problem, not a modeling problem. The model may be correct and still be unusable.

Measurement

A documented baseline, a metric matched to the error that actually costs something, and a route for feedback from the people affected. Without the first, improvement is an assertion. Without the second, the metric can improve while the outcome gets worse.

Cost of skipping it: a trading signal or forecasting model with no pre-deployment baseline cannot be defended in a budget review, because nobody can separate the model's contribution from the market conditions it happened to launch into.

Governance & Monitoring

Drift detection, a named owner with the authority to stop the system, and a deliberate decision about how much personal data the model actually needs. This is the dimension teams defer, because on day one nothing is wrong yet.

Cost of skipping it: covariate shift is silent. A credit or pricing model degrades gradually as the customer base changes, and without monitoring the first signal is usually a quarter of unexplained losses or a customer complaint that escalates.

The reason the scorecard caps the verdict on the weakest dimension rather than averaging is worth stating plainly, because it is the single most transferable idea in this portfolio. A project that scores twenty out of twenty-four with governance at one is not eighty-three percent ready. It is a project that will fail in a specific, predictable way, and the score is telling you where.

The Throughline: Five Artifacts, One Argument

This portfolio was not planned as a sequence. It became one because each artifact ended with a question that the next one had to answer, and the questions kept moving in the same direction: away from how the technology works and toward whether a particular use of it can be trusted.

01

The Evolution of AI: A Visual Timeline

Traced the field across five eras, including two AI winters. The useful part was not the chronology but the pattern inside it: the winters followed periods when capability was oversold relative to what could be demonstrated.

Which raised: how do you tell a real capability from an overstated one?

02

StudyBridge: Designing, Building, and Testing an AI Study Assistant

Answered it by building one. A deployed study assistant with a defined audience, explicit academic-integrity boundaries, and adversarial testing against those boundaries. Building it made clear how much of the work is scoping and constraint rather than modeling.

Which raised: how would you measure whether it actually helps real users?

03

Inside the Black Box: Neural Networks, Trust, and How to Measure What You Cannot See

Opened the mechanism with a working neuron you operate yourself, mapped the four core architectures to business uses, and built a trust and measurement framework for systems whose reasoning cannot be inspected directly.

Which raised: what does it look like when measurement and trust are absent?

04

Museum of AI Failures: A Walk-Through of How Machine Learning Breaks in Production

Six exhibits where the visitor breaks the systems personally: skewing a training set, launching a biased hiring model, watching a recommender forget. Every failure came from the data and the surrounding process, not from the algorithm.

Which raised: so how would you check a project before any of this happens?

05

The Readiness Question

The scorecard above. Twelve questions that would have caught every failure in Artifact 04 before it shipped, scored against the trust framework from Artifact 03, applied to the kind of scoped, constrained build that Artifact 02 turned out to require.

Which closes the sequence, at least for this course.

Where a Score Falls Short

A scorecard is a compression, and compressions lose things. Three limitations are worth naming before anyone treats a number out of twenty-four as a verdict.

First, the scoring weights are a judgment, not a measurement. Twelve questions at two points each treats every question as equally important, which is not true for every project. For a model that never touches an individual person, such as demand forecasting for inventory, the explainability dimension carries less weight than it does for credit or hiring. The dimension breakdown is more informative than the total for exactly this reason.

Second, the scorecard is answered by the team being scored. Self-assessment is optimistic by default, and the questions with the widest gap between intention and practice, particularly the recurring bias review and the named accountable owner, are the easiest ones to answer generously. Anything scoring above eighteen is worth having a second person answer independently.

Third, and most importantly, readiness is not permission. A project can score twenty-four and still be a project that should not exist, because the score says nothing about whether the use itself is appropriate. A well-governed, fully explainable, carefully monitored system for scoring people on characteristics they cannot change is a well-built system doing something that warrants a separate conversation. This tool checks whether a project is built responsibly. It does not check whether it should be built.

Process

Started by re-reading the four prior artifacts in this portfolio and pulling out every failure mode, framework, and open question they contained, then grouping them until a structure emerged. The grouping collapsed cleanly into four dimensions, which was the signal that the sequence had been making one argument rather than four separate ones. Drafted the twelve questions so that each one is answerable by a practitioner in under fifteen seconds and has a defensible ordering between its three options, then rewrote roughly half of them after finding that the first drafts asked about intentions ("do you plan to monitor for drift") rather than current state, which is the failure mode of most maturity assessments.

Built the scorecard from scratch in plain JavaScript with no frameworks and no build step, matching the approach used in Artifacts 03 and 04. Chose weakest-link scoring over a simple total after testing the tool against the scenarios from Artifact 04: a straight average let the cancer detector scenario score respectably, which would have made the tool wrong about the exact case that motivated it. Tested the interactive logic across edge cases, including partially completed dimensions, ties between two equally weak dimensions, and repeated resetting. Verified the finished page in a live browser with the console open rather than relying on a code review, a practice adopted after peer feedback in Workshop Five identified a script-ordering bug in Artifact 03 that a code-level check had not caught. Finally, designed the one-page PDF version so the questions survive outside the browser.

Tools & Technologies

HTML / CSS / JavaScript (no frameworks, no build step) · Python with Pillow and ReportLab for the cover image and the downloadable one-page PDF · Git and GitHub Pages for version control and hosting · Chrome DevTools console for live verification · AIML-500 course materials across all five workshops · Claude (Anthropic) for structure and code assistance.

Value Proposition

Most AI readiness material is written for organizations rather than projects, and arrives as a consulting framework with maturity levels that take a workshop to apply. This is twelve questions and about three minutes, aimed at one specific project, producing a named weakest dimension and a concrete next step rather than a maturity tier. For finance professionals and strategy leaders evaluating a vendor tool or an internal build, the questions are deliberately the ones that are uncomfortable to answer honestly, because those are the ones that predict what happens after launch.

Unique Value

Two things separate this from a generic checklist. The first is the weakest-link rule. Almost every readiness framework averages its dimensions, which produces a comfortable middling score for a project with one catastrophic gap. This one refuses to, and says so on the page, because the failures documented in Artifact 04 were all single-dimension failures inside otherwise competent projects.

The second is that every dimension is traceable to something demonstrated elsewhere in this portfolio rather than asserted here. A visitor who scores low on data foundation can go break a class-imbalanced classifier in Artifact 04 and see the mechanism, then come back. The scorecard is the index to four artifacts of worked examples, which is a different thing from a checklist that asks you to take its word for it.

Relevance

The gap this addresses is widening rather than closing. Model capability is increasingly available as a purchased service, which means the differentiator for most organizations is no longer whether they can build a model but whether they can tell a deployable one from a liability. That judgment sits with people who are not the modelers: risk officers, product owners, heads of function, people approving a budget line.

In finance specifically, the four dimensions map onto obligations that already exist. Data foundation and governance overlap with model risk management expectations. Explainability overlaps with adverse-action notice requirements in lending. Measurement is what a model validation function asks for. The scorecard does not replace any of those processes, but it gives someone without a modeling background a structured way to ask the first round of questions before a formal review begins, which is the point in the timeline where a project is still cheap to change.

Key Learnings

  • Building a synthesis forced a claim the individual artifacts had only implied. Grouping four artifacts of material into four dimensions was straightforward. Deciding that the weakest dimension caps the verdict was not, and that single decision is what makes the tool say something rather than describe something.
  • Questions about current state are far harder to write than questions about intent, and much more useful. The first draft asked what teams plan to do, which every team answers well. Rewriting toward what is true today made several questions uncomfortable, which was the correct outcome.
  • A bug that a code review missed and a peer caught changed how I verify work. The script-ordering error in Artifact 03 failed in every browser, every time, and passed a syntax check cleanly. Loading the page in a real browser with the console open is now the acceptance test, not the final formality.
  • Writing the limitations section was the part that clarified the tool most. Naming that readiness is not permission, and that a well-governed system can still be doing something that warrants a separate conversation, drew a boundary around what a scorecard can honestly claim.
  • Across five artifacts, the consistent finding is that the interesting problems in machine learning are rarely in the model. They are in the data, the measurement, and the question of who is accountable, which is also why this material transfers to people who will never train anything.
