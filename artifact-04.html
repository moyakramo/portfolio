ARTIFACT 04

Museum of AI Failures: A Walk-Through of How Machine Learning Breaks in Production

Data Quality & Bias Interactive Simulation AI Risk & Governance

Six exhibits, six machine learning systems that look successful right up until you turn one dial. Skew a training set, launch a biased hiring model, or watch a recommender quietly forget what it learned. Each exhibit is built from a real scenario in this course's data-challenges material, and each one ends with the same question: how would you have caught this before it shipped?

Step into Exhibit 01 ↓ Download the field checklist

Introduction

Most machine learning fails quietly. A model does not crash, it just gets slightly worse, or confidently wrong, or fair on the metric a team happened to check and unfair on the one nobody did. This course's material on data challenges lists more than a dozen ways this happens: availability, quality, labeling, privacy, bias, imbalance, scalability, versioning, integration, drift, annotation cost, real-time processing, interoperability. Reading that list is one thing. Watching a model fail because of it, in real time, under your own hand on the slider, is another.

This artifact is a small museum. Six exhibits, each one a machine learning system built to look successful right up until you turn one dial. Two of these exhibits (the cancer detector and the recommendation engine) are built directly from a graded scenario-coaching session for this course, where an AI coach walked through class imbalance, data drift, and explainability in a credit model. The rest come straight from this week's lesson outline of data challenges. Walk through in order, or jump to whichever failure mode is closest to a system you already work with.

Objective

Three goals. First, make the data challenges from this week's lesson something a visitor operates rather than reads, translating each concept (class imbalance, data drift, bias, data quality, explainability, privacy) into a small interactive scenario built from scratch in plain JavaScript. Second, root every exhibit in a concrete business or finance application, since a slider that only ever talks about cancer detection or streaming content will not stick with an audience of finance professionals and strategy leaders. Third, leave the visitor with something to take away: a checklist room and a downloadable one-page reference that turns six entertaining failures into a list of questions worth asking before any model reaches production.

Exhibit 01: The 99% Accurate Cancer Detector

A healthcare startup trains a classifier to detect a rare, aggressive cancer from medical imaging. The dataset: 50,000 healthy scans, 200 malignant ones, a 250-to-1 imbalance. That ratio is not a contrived example: rare-disease detection, fraud detection, and loan default all share this shape, a small handful of the cases that matter buried inside a mountain of the cases that do not.

Class Imbalance
A model that always predicts "healthy" would still be right 99.6% of the time, and would correctly identify 0 of the roughly 200 cancer patients in a population this size.

The actual deployment: 50,000 healthy scans, 200 malignant.

Healthy scans, correctly identified50,000 / 50,000
Cancer scans, correctly identified0 / 200

This model never flagged a single cancer patient. Its accuracy score never told anyone. Recall (the share of actual cancer cases it caught) was 0%. Precision was undefined, because it never made a single positive prediction to be right or wrong about.

Illustrative model, based on the class-imbalance scenario in this course's Data Challenge Coach transcript.

Where this shows up in business: anywhere the event you care about is rare, the same shape appears: fraud detection (a tiny fraction of transactions are fraudulent), loan default (most borrowers repay), and churn (most customers stay, until they do not). If a vendor pitches a fraud model on "99.7% accuracy" alone, the question this exhibit answers is the one to ask back: accuracy against what baseline, and what is the model's recall on the class it was actually built to catch?

Exhibit 02: The Model That Forgot

A streaming platform's recommendation engine has run well for three months. Then the platform launches a new content category, "Micro-Shorts," and within two weeks it is 15% of all views. The model was never trained on it. Nothing crashes. Recommendations just get a little worse, then a little worse again.

Data Drift
Week 0 of 6 (before launch)87
⚠ Data Drift Detected: input distribution has shifted since training

Illustrative animation, based on the covariate-shift scenario in this course's Data Challenge Coach transcript.

Under the hood, a recommender like this usually has two halves: a "wide" component that memorizes exact category IDs, and a "deep" component that generalizes through learned embeddings. A brand new category has no memorized weight in the wide half at all, and only a rough, borrowed guess in the deep half, built from other metadata like duration or genre. Both halves are guessing. The deep half is just guessing less blindly.

Where this shows up in business: wherever the world changes faster than the retraining cycle: a pricing model built before a new competitor entered the market, a demand forecast built before a product line launched, a fraud model built before a new payment method existed. Data drift is not a bug you fix once. It is a maintenance cost to budget for, which is why production models need monitoring, not just a launch date.

Exhibit 03: The Biased Hiring AI

A hiring platform trains a resume-screening model on ten years of a company's past hiring decisions. If those past decisions leaned heavily toward one group, the model does not correct for that on its own. It learns the pattern and repeats it, confidently and at scale.

Data Bias
Approval rate, male candidates62%
Approval rate, female candidates62%
Approval gap: 0 percentage points. A balanced training set produces a balanced outcome.

Illustrative model built to show the direction and mechanism of bias amplification, not a measurement from a real hiring dataset.

The mechanism here is exactly the one this week's material describes: bias can enter at data selection, at labeling, in algorithm design, in whoever tests the outputs, and in how results get interpreted afterward. A model is not biased because someone intended it to be. It is biased because nobody built a check for it.

Where this shows up in business: in lending, hiring, and underwriting, this is not hypothetical; public cases exist where automated screening amplified exactly this kind of historical skew. The practical takeaway for a hiring or credit leader is not "the vendor guarantees fairness." It is "what does the approval-rate gap look like across groups, and who checks it on a schedule, not just at launch."

Exhibit 05: The Black Box Loan

A lender uses a high-performing gradient-boosted model (XGBoost) to approve personal loans. It significantly outperforms the old manual process. It also cannot, on its own, tell a denied applicant why they were denied, which the law requires. A probability score is not a reason.

Explainability

Applicant #4471

Denied · probability of default 0.81

Illustrative reason codes, styled after SHAP feature-contribution output, built from the explainability scenario in this course's Data Challenge Coach transcript.

The technique behind this view is SHAP: for one specific applicant, it measures how much each individual feature pushed the prediction up or down relative to an average applicant, using an algorithm called TreeSHAP that runs fast enough to explain every application, not just a sample. It keeps the full performance of the underlying model instead of trading it away for a simpler, weaker one.

Where this shows up in business: this is the difference between "the model said no" and a compliant adverse-action notice: "denied primarily due to debt-to-income ratio and limited credit history." Any finance leader deploying a black-box model in a regulated decision (credit, insurance, hiring) should be asking whether this layer already exists, not whether it can be bolted on later.

The Checklist Room

Six exhibits cover six failure modes. This week's material lists more than a dozen. Click each item you would check before trusting a model in production; this is the list to leave with.

✓Data availabilityIs there enough relevant data for this specific use case?
✓Data qualityComplete, accurate, and consistent, or quietly full of gaps?
✓Labeling & annotation qualityCorrect, consistent labels, with audited annotators?
✓Data biasDoes the training data reflect who the model will affect?
✓Data imbalanceDoes the class you care about have enough examples to learn from?
✓Data driftIs anyone monitoring live inputs against the training distribution?
✓Privacy & securityAre GDPR, HIPAA, and equivalent constraints actually respected?
✓Integration & interoperabilityAre multiple data sources combined consistently?
✓VersioningCan you reproduce exactly what a model was trained on?
✓ScalabilityCan the pipeline handle deployment-scale volume, velocity, and variety?
✓ExplainabilityCan an affected individual get a specific, compliant reason?
✓Real-time processingDoes a batch-trained model need to adapt to streaming data?

For Leaders: Navigating Human Bias in AI

Every exhibit above traces back to a human decision. AI systems learn from data that people select, label, design algorithms around, test, and interpret, and human cognitive bias (the quiet, unnoticed shortcuts our brains use to make sense of a complicated world) can enter at any one of those five points. An AI system built entirely by well-meaning people can still learn and repeat a bias none of them intended, because bias is rarely a decision anyone makes on purpose. It is a gap nobody checked.

For a leader, the practical response is not a single audit before launch. It is a small set of habits, run on a schedule:

  • Diverse review, not diverse hiring alone. A team with varied backgrounds catches skew in training data and outputs that a homogeneous team tends to miss, precisely because they are looking for different things.
  • Scheduled bias audits, not launch-day audits. Exhibit 03's approval-rate gap does not stay fixed once a model ships; retraining on new data can quietly reopen it. Audit on a calendar, the way you would reconcile a ledger.
  • Feedback channels that reach the model, not just a complaints inbox. A denied applicant's appeal is a data point about the model, not only about their case.
  • Transparency as a default, not a crisis response. Communicating how a decision was made, before someone has to ask, builds the kind of trust a legal disclaimer cannot.

In a finance setting specifically, this looks like an underwriting or lending team reviewing approval-rate gaps across demographic groups every quarter, the same way Exhibit 03 lets you watch a gap widen or close on a slider, rather than waiting for a regulator or a lawsuit to ask the question first.

Where This Simulator Simplifies

Every number in these six exhibits, aside from the 50,000/200 imbalance and the Micro-Shorts figures pulled directly from this course's scenario-coaching transcript, is illustrative: chosen to make a mechanism visible, not measured from a real dataset. A real bias audit needs actual approval data broken out by protected class, not a slider. A real drift detector needs a live monitoring pipeline, not a six-week animation. Treat every exhibit here as a mental model, not a benchmark.

The stakes are not illustrative, though. A biased hiring model or a black-box loan denial affects a real person's income or opportunity, not a demo score. A museum exhibit can afford to be a little playful. A real deployment cannot: the same imbalance, bias, and explainability problems shown here for a few minutes of interaction are, in production, discrimination law, healthcare outcomes, and regulatory exposure.

Process

Started from this week's course material on data challenges in machine learning (availability, quality, labeling, privacy, bias, imbalance, scalability, versioning, integration, drift, annotation cost, real-time processing, interoperability) and from a graded scenario-coaching session with this course's AI chatbot, which walked through class imbalance in a cancer classifier, covariate shift in a recommender, and explainability in a credit model. Used those two chatbot scenarios as the direct source for Exhibits 01, 02, and 05, and built the remaining three exhibits (bias, data quality, privacy) from the lesson's outline of challenges. Coded each exhibit from scratch in plain JavaScript, no frameworks, then tested the interactive logic across edge cases: slider extremes, every checkbox on and off in any order, rapid repeated clicking on the drift animation. Wrote the checklist room and the leaders section to fold the full list of challenges back together, and designed a one-page field checklist in the site's visual style for download.

Value Proposition

Most discussions of data challenges in machine learning stay abstract: a list of terms, a paragraph of consequences. This artifact hands the reader six small, working failures instead, in the "show, don't describe" spirit of this portfolio: move a slider, watch a model fail, and the concept stops being a vocabulary word. For finance professionals and strategy leaders, every exhibit closes with the specific business application it maps to, and the checklist room turns six entertaining minutes into a list that survives past the browser tab: the questions worth asking before any model reaches production.

Key Learnings

  • Accuracy can hide total failure. Building Exhibit 01 made the recall problem impossible to un-see: a number that looks perfect and a model that catches nothing can be the same model.
  • Mechanism teaches faster than description. Writing the illustrative bias slider taught me more about why bias compounds through a pipeline than reading the lesson's list of entry points did on its own; building the demo forced me to make the mechanism concrete enough to code.
  • Explainability is a layer you add, not a trade-off you accept. TreeSHAP's speed is what makes Exhibit 05 realistic: a lender does not have to choose between an accurate model and a compliant one.
  • Writing for a non-technical, executive audience is a harder exercise than writing for a technical peer. Every exhibit needed one sentence a leader could repeat in a meeting without a caveat attached, which took several drafts to get honest and simple at the same time.
  • Six small pieces changed how I think about scope, compared to one large one. Each exhibit had to stand alone and make its point in under a minute, which is a different discipline than building a single deep interactive widget.
